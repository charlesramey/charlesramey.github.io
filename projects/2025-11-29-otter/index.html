Shelling Out the Fun: Quantifying Otter Interactions with Instrumented Enrichment Objects

December 2024 • Charles Ramey, Jason Jones, Kristen Hannigan, Elizabeth Sadtler, Jennifer Odell, Thad Starner, Melody Moore Jackson

Enrichment is a critical component of animal welfare in zoos and aquariums, providing mental stimulation and encouraging natural behaviors. However, designing enrichment for sea otters presents unique challenges. Otters are inquisitive and dexterous, but they are also incredibly destructive and live in a harsh aquatic environment. In this project, we explored the design of "ruggedized" instrumented objects to quantify how otters interact with digital enrichment devices.

Designing for "Destructive Users"

Unlike typical human-computer interaction (HCI) scenarios, our "users" (sea otters) interact with objects by biting, banging, and submerging them. This requires a level of physical ruggedness far beyond standard consumer electronics. We developed two custom-designed instrumented enrichment objects capable of surviving these high-impact interactions while logging data on the intensity and duration of use.

Study & Deployment

We deployed these devices with sea otters (including subjects Emma and Buck) to evaluate their engagement. The system utilized embedded sensors to track:

  • Interaction Duration: How long the otters engaged with the device.
  • Interaction Intensity: The force and frequency of manipulation (e.g., banging the object against surfaces, a natural foraging behavior for otters).

Figure 1: The ruggedized enclosure design protecting the internal sensors.

Results & Insights

Our pilot study demonstrated that the instrumented objects could successfully withstand the otters' rigorous play. While the otters did not necessarily "solve" the tool-use task in the traditional sense during the initial trials, the data quantified a high level of engagement.

The system successfully differentiated between different types of interactions, providing caretakers with objective metrics to assess the effectiveness of the enrichment program. This work lays the foundation for future "smart" enrichment devices that can adapt to an animal's behavior in real-time.

For more details, please refer to the full paper published in the Proceedings of the International Conference on Animal-Computer Interaction (ACI '24).
