AGILE: AGility Innovations Leveraging Electronics

December 2023 • Charles Ramey, Arianna Mastali, Arielle Pechette Markley, Rachel A Olson, Aayahna Herbert, Ting-Hui Lin, Brendan J Sietz, Adithya Muralikrishna, Thad Starner, Melody Moore Jackson

[cite_start]Canine agility is one of the fastest-growing dog sports in the world, involving high-speed navigation of obstacle courses by a handler and their dog[cite: 38]. [cite_start]While the sport provides cognitive and physical benefits, it carries significant risks: emerging research shows that over 40% of agility dogs will suffer an injury while training or competing[cite: 17]. [cite_start]Despite this, very little research has been conducted to quantify the performance and welfare of participating dogs[cite: 18].

[cite_start]The AGILE project aims to bridge this gap by leveraging wearable electronic sensors to measure external load factors, recognize activity patterns, and communicate these metrics to handlers[cite: 19]. [cite_start]Our goal is to employ these innovations to optimize performance and prevent injuries[cite: 42].

Wearable Sensor System

[cite_start]We developed a custom collar-worn activity monitor designed specifically for the rigors of agility sports[cite: 20]. [cite_start]The sensing package is built around an ESP32 microcontroller, which provides dual-core processing for simultaneous sensor sampling and WiFi streaming[cite: 119].

Key hardware features include:

  • [cite_start]Sensors: An MPU6500 6-DoF Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) and a BMP390 piezoelectric barometer to capture physical forces and pressure changes[cite: 120].
  • [cite_start]Power Management: A magnetic reed switch allows for soft power switching—handlers can activate the device simply by swiping a magnet near the collar[cite: 122, 127].
  • [cite_start]Mechanical Design: The electronics are housed in a 3D-printed Thermoplastic Polyurethane (TPU) enclosure mounted on a standard breakaway collar to ensure safety in case of snags[cite: 136, 138].

Smartphone Application

[cite_start]To make the data accessible to handlers, we designed a smartphone application based on interviews with amateur competitors, coaches, and veterinarians[cite: 87, 88]. [cite_start]The interviews revealed that handlers prioritize training feedback and injury prevention[cite: 93, 94].

The application features distinct tabs for performance tracking:

  • [cite_start]Dashboard: Displays a high-level overview of daily activities and data trends[cite: 112].
  • [cite_start]Calendar: Assists handlers in planning training routines and physical activities in advance[cite: 110].
  • [cite_start]Logbook & Data: Allows users to view specific activity metrics, repetition counts, and log notes or videos[cite: 111].

Automated Activity Recognition

A key innovation of the AGILE project is the ability to automatically recognize specific agility obstacles using machine learning. [cite_start]We validated the system with a pilot study involving dogs performing courses with jumps, tunnels, and A-frames[cite: 364].

[cite_start]Using a MINIROCKET-based classifier, we achieved an average accuracy of 85.90% in identifying obstacles from the sensor data[cite: 366, 519].

Our analysis revealed distinct sensor signatures for different obstacles:

  • [cite_start]Weave Poles: Distinct back-and-forth rotations visible in the gyroscope data[cite: 220].
  • [cite_start]Jumps: Recognizable plateaus in accelerometer magnitude while the dog is in midair[cite: 221].
  • [cite_start]Tunnels: Unique pressure features detected by the barometer as the dog enters and exits the enclosed volume[cite: 222].

Effort & Performance Analysis

[cite_start]We collected data from 22 trained agility dogs to quantify the "effort" required for different obstacles[cite: 20]. [cite_start]We calculated an effort metric by dividing the cumulative acceleration magnitude by the duration of the activity[cite: 205].

  • [cite_start]Highest Effort: The tunnel obstacle required the highest cumulative effort from the dogs[cite: 206].
  • [cite_start]Longest Duration: The weave poles were the longest activity on average[cite: 206].
  • [cite_start]Baseline Comparison: Interestingly, sprinting on a flat surface (baseline) often required higher intensity effort than navigating specific obstacles, likely because dogs must slow down to negotiate the technical elements of the course[cite: 238, 239].
